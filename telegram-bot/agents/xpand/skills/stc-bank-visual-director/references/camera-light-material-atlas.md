# STC Bank Camera, Light and Material Atlas
VERSION: 1.0

Use one named camera setup per prompt. The terms below are photographic decisions, not decorative keywords.

## Camera and perspective vocabulary
- Eye-Level Shot: camera at the subject or product reference height; use for trust and believable human interaction.
- Three-Quarter View: oblique horizontal azimuth showing front and side; use for depth without forcing a high or low angle.
- Eye-Level Oblique Shot: eye-level height plus oblique azimuth; useful for real merchant action.
- Low-Angle Shot: camera below the hero looking upward; gives controlled authority while preserving proportions.
- Worm’s-Eye View / Extreme Low-Angle Shot: camera near the support plane looking steeply upward; use sparingly for architectural scale and keep contact plane visible.
- High-Angle Shot: camera above the hero looking downward; clarifies hand, object and surface relationships.
- Bird’s-Eye View: elevated overview with a near-overhead tilt; use for a readable system of relationships, never as a flat diagram.
- Top-Down / Overhead Shot: optical axis close to perpendicular to the support plane; use for organized surfaces and direct contact actions.
- Elevated Three-Quarter View: raised camera with oblique azimuth; use for connected purple planes, product hierarchy and top/side material read.
- Over-the-Shoulder Shot: camera behind a person with shoulder framing the action; use when human context matters.
- First-Person POV: camera at the participant’s eye position; hands and object reach must fit the body and lens.
- Dutch Angle / Canted Frame: intentional camera roll; use only when instability or directional tension is the idea.
- Close-Up: tight framing around an action or relationship; not automatically macro.
- Macro Shot: high reproduction ratio showing real material and contact detail; use for card, hand, bevel or surface evidence.
- Wide Environmental Shot: broad frame where the location explains the benefit.
- Reflection-Led Shot: camera and hero remain real while a controlled surface reflection reveals secondary evidence; never use a fake duplicate.
- Compressed Long-Lens View: distant camera with long-lens compression; use for layered commercial depth, not to flatten every set.
- Forced Perspective: one coherent camera and distance relationship creates apparent scale; all shadows and contact must agree.

## Perspective systems
- Two-Point Perspective: horizontal direction families converge toward two vanishing points; preferred for connected purple architecture.
- Three-Point Perspective: verticals also converge because of a deliberate high or low viewpoint; use for architectural drama with restraint.
- Orthographic Projection: parallel rays with no normal photographic foreshortening; use only for a deliberately technical visual, not ordinary advertising photography.

Perspective is determined by camera position. Focal length changes framing and compression behavior; it does not magically create a new viewpoint. Specify height, looking direction, azimuth, tilt, distance, lens character, crop and focus target.

## Lighting language
- Broad soft key: large motivated source shaping the hero without hard artificial edges.
- Violet/magenta pool: broad architectural light on an upper-right or rear plane, not a neon outline.
- Negative fill: controlled dark surface that preserves graphite separation and local contrast.
- Restrained rim or edge reflection: a real source reflected on a bevel, glass edge or dark object.
- Fill and bounce: limited enough to retain dimensional shadow; never flatten the set.
- Falloff: describe how light loses intensity across the plane and into the lower frame.
- Contact shadow and ambient occlusion: required wherever objects meet a plane or hand.
- Specular highlight: a reflected source shaped by the material normal; never paint a white stripe onto an object.

## Material and reflection language
- Matte: broad low-intensity response with visible microtexture.
- Satin lacquer: controlled soft highlight with a readable roughness transition; not a mirror and not acrylic.
- Gloss lacquer: sharper reflection that follows the plane and source geometry.
- Graphite or black hardware: dark body with a restrained violet rim, readable bevel and grounded shadow.
- Glass: transparent depth, controlled edge highlight and reflections that do not become a second object.
- Brushed metal: directional streaking aligned with the grain, never random chrome.
- Fabric or paper: diffuse folds and soft occlusion; never mirror-like.

Reflections must reveal the environment and camera logic. Every bright highlight needs a plausible source, direction and surface normal. Keep color separation between violet planes, graphite objects, skin and any mint accent.
